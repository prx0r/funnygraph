## KEY ANALYTICAL MOVES (what to steal)

### 1. The grounded-absurd framework
Sean Lock = "grounded person + absurd person" (from Mighty Boah
discussions with Greg Davies). One voice grounds in logistics
("sponge bread feet... how'd you buy shoes for those?"), the other
answers with absurdism ("special bread shoe store... bread tunnels").
Lock does BOTH in every premise. The grounding makes the absurd
believable. The absurd makes the grounding funny.

**Maps to our data:** juxtapose operator (+.0047 lift) + literalize.
The grounding is the literalize; the absurd is the juxtapose. The
tension between them is the engine.

### 2. The 'sixth option' superpower
"His premises make you go 'he could go five different places with
this' and you're trying to work it out... he will always surprise
you and always do the sixth option out of five."

Contrast: Michael McIntyre follows a "great recipe" — you see the
premise, you know where it's going, you laugh at the craft. Lock
combines "weird flavors" — you don't know what he's making until
you take a bite.

**Maps to our data:** this is the garden_path operator. The audience
is led toward expectation X, Lock delivers Y. The "sixth option" is
the Y that wasn't in the audience's probability distribution.

### 3. The observational comedy skip
Normal observational comedy: recognition laugh → extrapolation laugh
→ extreme laugh (three stepping stones). Lock SKIPS the stepping
stones. He does the observation, then jumps straight to the extreme.
He knows audiences know the rhythm well enough that he can skip it.

**Maps to our data:** this is the STRUCTURE of observational comedy
mapped to our type board. Lock's version: the audience's trained
pattern-recognition lets the comedian compress the structure. The
skip IS the joke.

### 4. The reality bridge (Adam Bloom's concept)
"A gradual change from being in a believable place and then merging
into fantasy without there being a noticeable join."

Lock starts in the mundane ("I hate recycling"), then bridges to the
surreal ("swan's toilet... he'll break both your arms"). The bridge
is invisible. The audience doesn't notice the moment reality broke.

**Maps to our data:** this is the escalation operator with the
benign frame keeping the audience safe during the transition. The
bridge is the mechanism that makes BV work at surreal distances.

### 5. The protected vs unprotected self
"The protected self is almost never what you want to show on stage
cuz it's boring... you unprotect yourself as the weird silly bizarre
goofball human that you are. When we go and see comedy we want to
see the unprotected self."

Lock's panel persona oscillates: protected (grumpy old man face,
Jack D energy) → unprotected (elastic face, Green Goblin smile,
goofball chaos) → back to protected. The oscillation IS the engine.

**Maps to our data:** this is the modulator_range! Lock's modulators
would be: grumpy-baseline → chaotic-escape → deadpan-return. The
range between protected and unprotected is the comedian's unique
signature.

### 6. The practical effect theory
Films: do a practical effect next to CGI so the brain buys the
practical, then buys the CGI. Lock: do a genuine improv riff next
to written material so the brain buys the improv, then buys the
written stuff. "It all feels real."

**Maps to our data:** this is the meta operator — the comedian
manipulating the audience's authenticity-detection system. The
improv is the "practical effect"; the written material is the
"CGI." The audience can't tell which is which.

### 7. The panel show as safety net
"On panel shows he has six people to do the leg work... Jimmy is
doing the statistics and the premise at the top... he just gets
to immediately jump to Stage N. He gets to do his reality bridging
moment."

In standup: "he has to do the boring stuff, the premises, the
groundwork of the logic of a bit before he gets to take off and fly."

**Maps to our data:** this explains why Lock's panel appearances
score higher than his standup on our boards — the format removes
the premise-clarity burden. The panel format IS a comedy mechanism.

### 8. The Jimmy Carr double-act
Jimmy Carr and Sean Lock were "in a double act for 20 years."
Jimmy supplies premises and quick wit; Lock supplies "flurries of
comic inspiration that are raw and need to be shaped." Jimmy
sculpts; Lock generates. Mickey Flanagan = Lock's "biggest
cheerleader" — his laughter signals the audience to get on board.

**Maps to our data:** this is the split_knowledge mechanism at
the FORMAT level. The audience follows the laughter of comedians
who understand what Lock is doing. The cheerleader-laugh is the
alliance signal, just like the audience laugh in Louis's machine.

### 9. The Innocence thesis
"Sean kept it [the spark of madness]. He didn't have to be the
smartest comedian in the room he only had to be the funniest."

Robin Williams quote: "You're only given a little spark of Madness.
And if you lose that, you're nothing."

Lock's comedy felt INNOCENT — not trying to be clever, not trying
to impress. "It's just trying to make them laugh in the same way
you would try to make your friends laugh on the school ground."

**Maps to our data:** this is the BV framework at its purest.
The violation is real (the content is absurd/offensive). The benign
frame is the innocence — the audience knows he's playing, not
attacking. The innocence IS the benign frame.

### 10. The deadpan misdiagnosis
"He unfairly gets a reputation as a deadpan comic and he's not in
any way. He's so alive. His face is moving all the time. His voice
is fluctuating. He's doing crazy stuff. His body is always in motion.
But it's because his RESTING FACE is that [grumpy] — like 'oh yeah,
no it's the deadpan guy' — and then every bit is him lunging and
doing weird elastic stuff with his body."

The resting face sets the baseline. Every departure from the
baseline is a joke. The deadpan isn't the delivery — it's the
LAUNCHPAD.

**Maps to our data:** this is the delivery_archetype distinction.
Lock's archetype isn't "deadpan" — it's "grumpy-baseline with
elastic escape." The archetype name matters because it changes
what we expect from the timing data.

## TONE NOTES (what makes CwE work)

1. **Personal obsession as frame.** "I was completely obsessed...
   I probably watched the first 13 seasons of 8 Out of 10 Cats five
   or six times when I was 17, 18 years old." The narrator IS a fan
   who studied the material. Not a critic. Not a professor.

2. **Interview-as-evidence.** The narrator interviews another comic
   (Guy Montgomery) who also loved Lock. Two fans comparing notes.
   The interview clips ARE the analysis, not decoration.

3. **Theory naming without jargon.** "Reality bridging" (from Adam
   Bloom's book). "Protected vs unprotected self." "Practical effect
   theory." The narrator NAMES the mechanisms but uses accessible
   language. No academic vocabulary.

4. **The clip IS the text.** The narrator doesn't explain over clips.
   They play a clip, pause, analyze, resume. The clip and the
   analysis are the same object.

5. **The deeper reading arrives organically.** "On a deeper level,
   Louis is actually trying to make a comment on the ruthlessness
   of the economy." (from the earlier GOLD.md). Here: "it felt
   innocent... it reminds me of this Robin Williams quote." The
   theory emerges from the material, not the other way around.

6. **Honest uncertainty.** "I've struggled with this as a research
   assignment... everyone gives examples of really clean personas
   and they go 'Jack D is grumpy' — Sean is so hard to make sense
   of." The narrator admits when the analysis is hard. That's
   credibility, not weakness.

7. **Chapter structure as essay skeleton.** 27 chapters. Each
   chapter is a LENS, not a topic. "The sixth option superpower."
   "Grounding the absurd." "The protected vs unprotected self."
   The chapter titles ARE the thesis statements.
